MONDAY
A second send that isn't a carbon copy of Thursday: the plan, the pitch and every way it could go wrong

The idea: Thursday stays the issue that catches Charlestonians up on what changed. Monday becomes the issue that helps them do something with the week.

Built from your own material, ranked verdicts, the week's best events, one number explained, and a reader question. Piloted for six Mondays before it earns a permanent slot.

The appetite for a shorter, different issue has been tested in pieces. A full Monday issue hasn't.

The split

Two issues, two jobs

Thursday: catch up

  • What opened, what closed, what's being built
  • The news lead with a hook
  • Development Watch and ICYMI
  • Where Am I?, polls, partner slots

Monday: do something

  • Your verdict on something readers can act on
  • The week's best things to do, in one scan
  • One number, explained
  • A question readers answer, with last week's result

The test for any Monday item: would a reader use it this week? If it's news, it belongs on Thursday. If it's a verdict, a plan or a reaction, it belongs on Monday.

The menu

What goes in a Monday issue

1

The Ranked List Lead

One verdict a week. Best breakfast sandwich under $12, the five patios worth the wait, the definitive list of Charleston pizza. You rank, you explain, you pick a winner and a surprise.

Why it fits: verdicts are your best-performing lead type, and on Thursday they compete with breaking news for the top spot. On Monday they get it every week. It also gives readers something to argue about, which is free engagement.

Production: you supply the opinion and the shortlist. The pack drafts the structure and the blurbs around your picks, using only what you tell it. Your voice stays yours.

Watch out: a ranking that includes a sponsor or a sponsor's competitor needs a clear rule (see risks).

2

The Week Ahead Backbone

The best eight or so things happening Monday through Sunday, plus the live music highlights, in one tight scan. Dinner Club date and Where Am I? teaser can ride along.

Why it fits: the data already exists in your Events and Live Music tables, and Monday morning is when people plan. It's also the part that can be most automated.

Watch out: events historically don't earn clicks on Thursday. The Week Ahead is the utility spine, not the click engine. The ranked lead carries the click-to-open number. It also makes event-data quality more visible, since the curation clean-up problem (out-of-town and junk listings) would now show up twice a week.

3

Monday Math Curiosity

One number, explained in three short paragraphs. $13.5 million. 165 rooftop seats. A $20 million path that doesn't exist yet. The number is the hook, the explanation is the payoff.

Why it fits: your dollar-figure and count headlines do well, and it's the cheapest way to produce curiosity-gap content.

Watch out: every figure needs a source you can name, and you audit for unsourced flourishes. Build these from public records and filings you can cite, not from re-telling someone else's reporting.

4

The Poll and the Result Participation

One question Monday, last week's answer revealed with a one-line reaction from you. Think of it as a smaller, weekly cousin of the Reader Census.

Why it fits: it gives readers something to do without asking them to join anything. The paid community never produced reader posts (44 of 47 posts were yours), but a one-tap poll has a much lower barrier.

Watch out: this is the least proven piece. I haven't seen response numbers for the Census polls, so this is the one to measure first.

5

Dinner Club and One Partner Slot Revenue

The next Dinner Club date and a single sponsor placement. Dinner Club is the conversion engine that actually works, and a Monday planner audience fits restaurants, events and local services.

Watch out: one slot, clearly labeled, no stacking. See the money section below.

6

The Backstory Rotating, optional

A short history of one Charleston place. Evergreen, so you can bank several ahead of time and they never go stale.

Why it fits: readers clearly love knowing the city (Where Am I? exists for a reason), and history pieces don't depend on a news cycle.

Watch out: same sourcing rule as everything else. Every claim traces to a source or gets cut.

What it looks like

A sample Monday run order

Aim for a four-minute read, short enough that a Monday morning reader finishes it.

Subject-line rule: same playbook as Thursday. Tease the verdict, don't describe it. Keep the palm tree prefix so the brand reads the same in the inbox. If Monday ever has the same subject energy as Thursday's lead, one of them is wrong.

What stays Thursday only: openings and closings, Development Watch, ICYMI, the news lead, Where Am I? and the weekly live music block. Nothing moves, so Thursday's habit and its numbers stay clean.

Production

How it would get made

Thursday's issue is built Wednesday at noon from six story links you drop in a row. Monday needs its own, lighter pipeline, assembled Sunday and sent Monday at 7 AM.

Time cap: I'd set 45 minutes a week as the ceiling. If Monday needs more, that's a signal to shrink the issue, not to work harder. It should run in the cloud under your subscription, with no Mac required and no metered billing, and publish through the same queue your other pages use.

Why it matters beyond CHS: design Monday so roughly 80% assembles itself and you have a second, repeatable edition to show as part of the New NEXT playbook. A two-send setup with one automated edition is a concrete module, not a theory.

Money

The sponsor angle, honestly

A second send doubles the number of slots you could sell, and a Monday planning audience suits restaurants, events and services. That's the upside.

The catch: as far as I can tell, your bottleneck is buyers, not inventory. More slots without more demand can quietly cheapen the ones you already sell. So I'd treat Monday as a way to give existing partners a second touch, or a lower-commitment first step, rather than as new inventory to sell cold. Decide the policy before the pilot: either Monday is bundled into packages as a bonus placement, or it's priced separately, but not both ways.

Risks

Everything that could go wrong

Likelihood is my judgment, not a measurement. Where I can name a protection, it's in bold.

Highest concern

1. Fatigue and unsubscribes

Your list is healthy because the Thursday habit is strong. A second weekly email is the single most common reason people leave a list they like. Even a small bump in unsubscribes on an 11K list is permanent, because you can't win those readers back.

Protection: pilot to people who raise their hand, not the whole list. Beehiiv should let you send to a segment, so a Thursday heads-up (“Want Mondays too?”) builds the pilot audience and lets you measure demand before committing. Compare unsubscribes per send against your normal Thursday rate (I didn't pull that number, so grab it first).

High concern

2. Thursday gets weaker

If Monday trains readers to skim, or to feel they already got their Charleston fix, Thursday's 71–74% opens and 8%+ click-to-open could slip. Thursday is the asset. Monday exists to serve it, not the other way around.

Protection: make Thursday's open rate and CTOR the main scoreboard. Opens on Monday can look fine while Thursday quietly erodes. Also, your own data says opens and CTOR settle by the following Wednesday, so judge each week's pair after that, not on Monday afternoon.

High concern

3. Your time and the New NEXT priority

You're the only operator. New NEXT is the immediate priority, and every hour spent on Monday is an hour not spent there. The risk isn't the first week; it's week eleven, when Monday is a standing obligation you resent.

Protection: the 45-minute cap, a hard pilot end date, and a rule that you can cancel a Monday with a one-line “no issue” note without guilt. Build the pipeline so a missed week costs nothing, as Thursday's already does.

Medium concern

4. Monday becomes a copy of Thursday with the serial numbers filed off

The pull is strong: a closing happens Sunday night, you'll want it in Monday. Once news leaks in, the two issues blur and readers stop knowing why there are two.

Protection: the one-line test (would a reader use this week?) and a firm rule that news stays Thursday. If something breaks big, it can be a one-line pointer to Thursday, not a Monday story.

Medium concern

5. The click engine is thin

Your best click-to-open lead types are verdicts and curiosity gaps. Monday has exactly one lead slot, so one weak ranked list drags the whole issue. The Week Ahead, the largest section, historically doesn't earn clicks.

Protection: run the same lead-hook gate you use on Thursday for the ranked list, and keep a bank of four or five verdict ideas ahead of time so you're never forced to run a flat one.

Medium concern

6. Ranked lists create enemies and conflicts

A verdict that ranks a business below another will annoy someone, and if that business is a partner or a prospect it gets awkward fast. A ranking that places a paying sponsor first looks bought.

Protection: write one clear rule now. For example, sponsors can appear in a ranking only if they genuinely earn it, are never ranked above where you'd put them otherwise, and a partner category is skipped the week that partner runs. Put the rule in writing before the first list.

Medium concern

7. Deliverability and engagement signals

Mailbox providers watch how people react to each send. A second email that gets weak engagement can pull down inbox placement for Thursday too. Gmail clipping is also a live constraint: your Thursday issues already run 86–91% of Gmail's size limit. Monday must stay far lighter.

Protection: keep Monday short (the four-minute target helps), watch Thursday's open rate for a drop that tracks the Monday sends, and keep the engaged-only pilot audience, which also protects sender reputation.

Medium concern

8. Sourcing and accuracy load

Monday Math and The Backstory both make factual claims, and you audit for anything unsourced. A second weekly issue doubles the number of facts that can be wrong, and a wrong number published to 11K people is hard to take back.

Protection: every number and date carries a source line in the draft, and anything without one is cut. Prefer public records and filings you can cite directly over re-telling other outlets' reporting, which also keeps Monday clearly original.

Medium concern

9. Event data quality becomes twice as visible

The junk-listing problem (out-of-town events, wrong venues, missing start times) has needed repeated fixes. A Week Ahead on Monday puts the same data in front of readers a second time, with less time to catch bad rows between sends.

Protection: a spot-check pass is part of the Sunday routine, and the existing no-confirmed-start-time rule applies to Monday too.

Lower concern

10. Poll fatigue and weak response

A weekly poll can go stale quickly, and I haven't seen response numbers for your Census polls. If response is low, the participation piece becomes dead weight.

Protection: measure responses per Monday for the first three weeks. If it's weak, replace it with a second verdict or a reader-submitted pick.

Lower concern

11. Holiday weeks muddy the read

A pilot that runs through Thanksgiving and early December mixes real signal with seasonal swings in opens and events.

Protection: compare each Monday to the same week's Thursday, not to an abstract baseline, and treat Thanksgiving week as its own data point. It's also the best natural week for a Monday “week ahead” issue.

Lower concern

12. Brand drift

The voice that works is witty, insider and a little cheeky. A utility-heavy Monday can read flat if the voice goes missing.

Protection: the lead and the poll reaction must be in your voice. Everything else can be structured. Same palette, same header, so it reads as the same publication.
