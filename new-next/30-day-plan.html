01

Three registers, one a week

Who governs what, settled Oct 1. Two sources run this plan and they no longer split evenly. Natasha Walstra governs what gets written — the register system below, the story requirement, the weekly mix, and the scoreboard in section 12. Richard van der Blom governs mechanics and measurement — hook taxonomy, ranking signals, what a link costs, the golden hour, the comment ladder, composing in-app. Algorithm facts, not content strategy. Where they disagree, she wins on what to optimize for and he wins on how the machine behaves: he describes what the algorithm rewards, she describes what is worth caring about. His semi-annual algorithm report is being bought in November — section 03 gets a refresh pass then

Walstra's framework, from the Sep 30 webinar transcript. Her own origin for it: every other framework paralysed her, so she asked what she was really trying to do — build a network of people who know, like and trust her — and found that each of those three words has its own kind of post. This is the spine of the plan. What a post is about matters less than what job it does for the reader.

Know
Your expertise
Industry insight, point of view, what you want to be known for. "How I did a thing," never "how to do a thing" — her sharpest distinction, because anybody can write a how-to now and nobody believes a human wrote it
Trust
Social proof: what others say about you
Client success stories, recommendations, "how my client did a thing." Third-party by definition. Earned media counts too: speaking, podcasting, collaborations
Like
Who you are beyond the work
Values tested in a real story, tied forward to how you operate now. The gatekeeper, not the dessert — her line: if people don't like you, they won't pay attention to the Know and Trust content at all

Her current ratio is one of each per week, down from two Know, two Trust, one Like, because of AI saturation and what she calls the intimacy recession. Three posts a week maps onto that exactly. Until the sponsor post clears, Trust is unavailable, so the week runs one Know and two Like.

The diagnosis, corrected twice on Oct 1. First read: one-note on subject, too many posts about measurement and machinery. Wrong — subject repetition is the asset. Narrow subject is how people learn what you're for. Second read, written into this document the same morning and also wrong: that Trust means bringing your own evidence. It doesn't. Your own figures about your own work are Know. Trust requires somebody else's voice.

What's true of the first five posts. All five are Know, and all five are the right form of Know — "how I did a thing" rather than "how to," the distinction she says most people miss. That part is already working, by instinct rather than design. Zero Like, and Like is the gatekeeper.

What the inversion exposed, and it is the reason for doing it. Tagged by register, the twelve briefs come out nine Know, two Trust, one Like — and the single Like post sits in Week 4. The plan back-loaded the only gatekeeper post to the last week of the month. Inside the lane system that was invisible, because the lanes are assigned by day of the week and every one of those nine Know posts wore a different lane label.

Trust is genuinely gated, and the answer isn't to redefine it. No New NEXT clients means no client stories. Her prescription for someone in that position is to lean on personal experience and ordinary relatable moments: when Trust isn't available you spend on Like, you don't manufacture Trust. Two doors are open. Earned media counts the day a podcast or speaking slot happens. And CHS Happenings sponsors are clients of a business he runs, so a sponsor's campaign numbers are real "how my client did a thing" content — the Week 3 post waiting on a sponsor's blessing, worth more than this plan had it.

The failure mode she names, and the one closest to home. A Know post with no story around it gets scrolled past now, because readers assume AI wrote it. At 129-135 impressions a post, a flat mechanism-explainer is exactly the shape people skip. The fix is a story around the mechanism, not a better hook.

02

The three-question test

Run any candidate insight post through this before you write it. It's the tool that generates the lane, so you're not dependent on this document once the 30 days are up.

1
What did this actually cost you, or a client, before you figured it out?
A number helps. Dollars, months, a deal, a thesis you ran on for two years
2
What were you doing before you knew better?
The actual mistake, not the polished retrospective version of it
3
Would you be comfortable if someone asked a follow-up in the comments?
If the answer is no, that's the post. Discomfort is what separates a real confession from a tidy lesson dressed up as vulnerability. And it has to be currently uncomfortable, not "here's what I learned twenty years ago"

The rules of the road

This section is van der Blom's, and it is the half of him that stays load-bearing. Everything here is algorithm mechanics: what the ranking signals weigh, what a link costs, the hook multipliers, the posting window. None of it tells you what to write — that is section 01 now. Cadence
3x/week · Tue / Wed / Thu
Sits inside his 2-4 optimal band, down from 5-6 in prior years. Post mid-morning, 10-11 ET, and hold the window
The golden hour
Cultivate depth, not speed
Depth of engagement is the ★★★★★ ranking signal; who engages first is only ★★, so early likes from familiar faces can't rescue a weak post. Be present 60-90 min to turn comments into threads — a reply that triggers 2+ more is what counts, a flat "great post" costs you. Reply inside 30 minutes, with substance: posts whose author replies that fast get 64% more comments and 2.3x more views (Oct 13 newsletter). A "thanks" does not count; add an opinion, a question or a new angle
Format
Set by register
Like posts: a real photo of you, several real images, or video. Know and Trust posts: text plus one real screenshot. Oct 16 teaser: personal stories with a selfie reached 19,000 (+21%) against 16,000 without; expert posts do best as text plus a single image. Original photos and real screenshots earn +30% because AI can't fake them. Video under 90 seconds. Carousels stay off until the November report settles them (his data has now pointed both ways). Polls are dead at 0.07%
Reach vs followers
Face gets seen, expertise gets followed
Oct 16 teaser, 120,000+ posts: followers per 1,000 people reached were 1.1 for a personal story, 1.4 with a selfie, 4.8 for an expert post. Per post the gap is smaller: 31 followers against 26. Run both registers and anchor the personal ones to the machine. At this account size followers are too noisy to steer by, so the scoreboard below does not change. Use the post text, never his infographic: its numbers do not match the text. Nov 3 puts a clean multiplier on the reach side: a personal story pulls 1.39x your average.
Collaboration
No co-authored posts
Oct 16 teaser: LinkedIn collaborative posts land at 42% of a creator's normal reach, 49% of engagement and 18% of follower growth. Trust comes from a client’s own sentence inside your post, not the co-author feature. Contested as of Nov 3: the Growth Blueprint calls collaborative posts the fastest legitimate route to a new audience, since the post publishes into every co-author’s feed. It offers no reach figure against van der Blom’s 42%. It does add the caveat that the lift only appears when the collaborator’s network is genuinely different from yours — which may be the whole explanation for the 42%, since most people collaborate with peers. No change. Open question for the Nov 16 report. Tagging someone (Matt McGarry, Oct 21) is a different thing and stays fine
Links
A cost you pay on purpose
One in-body link cuts median reach 18.8%, and the first-comment dodge is dead (comments with links suppressed up to 80%). So no link on reach posts; link on conversion posts and accept the hit
Topic discipline
Anchor insight to the topic
Oct 2026: on-topic posts reach 65% outside your network; personal storytelling only 30%, staying 70% inside. Your inside network is legacy contacts from a former career, not this audience. So every confession must be about the machine, not just about you — then it travels as on-topic. Only 10% of initial distribution goes to connections now, down from 70% in 2024. Nov 3 adds the headline number: your track record predicts about half your reach. A post off your topics is reach you already earned and handed back, which makes drift a more expensive mistake than a single weak post
Write it in-app
Type, don't paste
Composing directly in LinkedIn is worth +22% vs pasting from an AI tool, and even Word costs 9%. Correlational and confounded, but cheap to comply with — and retyping forces a last edit pass into your own voice. Never let anything but you write a comment: AI-flagged comments lose 88% and leave a 15% penalty on your next genuine one. This rules out publishing through AuthoredUp. Its editor and its analytics are worth having — the post-performance read on Oct 27 came out of that family of tooling — but its emails push you to publish from its composer, which is pasting by another name. Draft anywhere, paste nowhere: the last typing pass happens in the LinkedIn box
Engagement weights
The DM send is the only one that rose
Oct 13 newsletter, relative to a first-degree like: save 10-12x (was 14-16), instant repost 8-10x (was 10-12), DM send 8-10x (was 4-6), comment 6-8x (was 8-10), repost with thoughts 1-1.5x. Write for the save and the forward: a real number, a real case. His numbers, no stated method, so treat them as a direction
Sequencing
Story one day, expert the next
From his Oct 27 newsletter, and the one genuinely new thing in it. v38 already had that personal stories out-reach expert posts while expert posts convert better. This is the move that exploits both: the story pulls in people who would never stop for a framework, and that wider warmer audience is who LinkedIn shows your NEXT post to. So publish the expert post the day after the story, while you are still in their feed. Story does the reach work, expertise does the conversion work. Your own evidence: Oct 22, a story, reached 2,683 people at 71% out-of-network. Five days passed before the next post. That window is the thing to stop wasting
One story, one point
Three takeaways means three posts
Open inside the scene, not in a classroom. Start from a moment — a call, a mistake, a decision you got wrong — never from the lesson. Land it on a single insight. Specific beats inspirational
A bad stretch
Judge over weeks, not days
Reach has been rough platform-wide for weeks, Top Voices included, and he says LinkedIn is adjusting daily. Read your own soft numbers against that before concluding anything about a post. Do not buy your way out: no boosting to patch a dip, no Premium bought for reach, no pods — coordinated engagement breaks the rules and the systems are built to catch it. He is running a boosting test himself, measuring not the boosted posts but whether paying for one changes how the organic posts around it are treated. Results pending; worth watching
Length
100 to 199 words
Nov 3 infographic. That band is the sweet spot; posts of 300+ words reach only 0.88x. The mechanism is the next rule — a long post is a post fewer people finish. This one fights the plan. The five-beat outlines in section 05b, the specimen in section 10 and every post published so far all run well past 300 words. Treat 100 to 199 as the number to write toward and 300 as the line you need a reason to cross. Noted honestly: his own posts routinely break it, which is either an allowance for established accounts or a rule he does not follow himself
Completion
The heaviest signal in 2026
Nov 3 infographic, and the most consequential line in it. Of everything LinkedIn weighs, whether people reach the end now counts for most. It reframes this whole section: the length rule, one story one point, and write the way you talk are the same instruction from three angles. Write to be finished. A post nobody completes is not rescued by a good hook
The cut
Two line breaks can end the preview at 60 characters
Nov 3 infographic, and a trap aimed straight at your style. You write in period breaks and short lines, which is right for readability and dangerous here: a double line break near the top can truncate the feed preview at roughly 60 characters instead of around 200. Keep the first two or three lines tight together and spend the entire preview on the hook before you let the post breathe
Selling
Wrap the launch in a story
Nov 3 infographic. A straight launch post reaches 0.55x, about half. The two formats people expect to travel furthest — launches and collab posts (42%) — are the two that travel least. When New NEXT has something to sell, the announcement is a story with the offer inside it, not an announcement. And pick the goal before the format: reach, saves and sales are three different posts, and the save format is the list or framework at 1.85x more saves
Hashtags
Three at most, ideally none
From the Oct 2026 Growth Blueprint, and the plan had no rule at all. Hashtags were demoted from a reach driver to a weak topic hint. Posts with none currently out-reach posts with them by 5 to 10%, and ten or more risks a 30 to 50% penalty. You used two on the May relocation post. Default to zero
Bundled links
One link costs, four or more may gain
The third option this section was missing. A single in-body link still costs about 18.8% of median reach. But posts carrying four or more outbound links reportedly deliver 3 to 5x higher median reach than single-link posts, because the system reads a post linking to five reports as curation rather than a funnel. This is the shape of your book curation drop (section 08): five links in the body, not one, and not in the comments. Two separate analyses, roughly 1.3 million and 400,000 posts. Treat as promising, not settled
Feed diversity
You are ranked against the other posts in their feed
New, and in tension with the rule above it. LinkedIn now scores the top 10 to 20 posts queued for a reader as a set and down-ranks ones that are conceptually identical to others, even good ones. Topic discipline still wins you the right audience. But inside that audience’s feed you are competing with everyone else writing about newsletters and systems that morning. The way out is the thing you already have: a proprietary number nobody else can post. Not an opinion about newsletters, your own click report. That is the diversity candidate, and it is also the statistic hook
Substance over mechanics
The only post type of eight that gained
From his late-October study. Posts carrying both a concrete detail and lived experience went from +6% to +14% after LinkedIn’s 2026 changes — the one gainer in the set. Posts with neither fell from -11% to -28%. Everything built to manufacture engagement lost; the format that hands people something real to react to won. This is the first measured upside in this section — every other rule here is a penalty to avoid — and it puts a number on what section 01 and the three-question test already ask for. Method: the same creators measured against their own average, before and after. He cannot see which posts LinkedIn actually flagged, so he calls it direction, not proof. Four of the eight types are still unpublished and land in the November report
Asking
Never end on an instruction
Keyword CTAs (“comment GUIDE”) are the most punished pattern on the platform. His late-October study puts them at +18% before LinkedIn’s 2026 changes and -45% after — the biggest swing among the eight post types he measured. The July read was +9% to -52%; different windows, same verdict. The caveat this rule used to carry is gone. He now has a number for every other ask too: “drop your thoughts,” “save this,” “let me know” went from -4% to -20%, and he says the polite question tacked on the end is caught in that. End on a question the reader answers to themselves, not one that asks them for a reply. Any hint of a sales follow-up cuts engagement about 75%
Scoreboard
Recognition comments, signups, profile views
Comment quality means readers describing their own version of your mistake, not compliments. Plus one real downstream number: signups at jimmyfarrell.com. Saves still carry the most weight (10-12x a like), and they are visible on the Most recent post performance page, so count them rather than guessing — this line had been carrying the old error since v17 and section 12 was fixed on Oct 27 while this one was not. The format that earns them is the list or framework, at 1.85x
03c

The AI search layer

Added Nov 3, and it is the largest gap this plan had. Everything else in this document treats LinkedIn as a feed: you post, the algorithm decides who sees it, the post ages out in three days. That is one of two things LinkedIn now is. The second is that it has become the place AI assistants go when somebody asks a professional question, and that layer does not expire.
The finding
LinkedIn is the most-cited domain for professional questions
From an analysis of 325,000 prompts run across ChatGPT, Gemini, Google AI Overviews, AI Mode, Copilot and Perplexity, January to June 2026. About 11% of all AI answers cite LinkedIn, and for ChatGPT it moved from roughly 11th place overall to first on professional queries. The reason is structural: LinkedIn is the only place on the internet where a claim is attached to a name, a photo, a work history and an employer
Articles beat posts
63% of cited LinkedIn URLs
An article is cited roughly 3x as often as a profile and 1.5x as often as a post. It has a permanent URL; a feed post does not. The catch: a standalone article reaches about 0.4x what a normal post reaches in the feed. You will publish one, watch the impressions, and conclude it failed. It is not competing for today’s feed. It is competing to be the answer in six months
What gets cited
Specific, original, and not viral
The median cited post has 15 to 25 reactions. The AI is not reading engagement, it is reading relevance. 95% of cited posts are original rather than reshares. Over half of what gets cited is knowledge-sharing that answers a real question. About 75% of cited authors had posted at least five times in the previous four weeks. And named individuals take 91.7% of citations against company pages. Your posts run 3 to 11 reactions. The bar here is lower than the bar you have been failing to clear in the feed
What this means for you
One article a month, written as an answer
The recommendation. 500 to 2,000 words, answering one question your buyer would actually type: how a one-person newsletter gets built, what a local newsletter really costs to run, how to tell whether your ads are lying to you. Not a repackaged post. The thing you have that almost nobody writing about this has is two and a half years of your own numbers. This is the one item here that is a genuine addition to the month rather than a tweak to it, so it does not start until the catch-up week is cleared
Your profile is read by four audiences now
One of them was miscasting you. Tested and fixed Nov 6
Humans, the feed algorithm, LinkedIn’s own AI search, and outside LLMs. All of them read your headline and the opening of your About section first. Tested Nov 6, and the hypothesis held. The old headline carried “30+ years of business development, 725+ deals closed” — true, the strongest credential you own, and the phrase most likely to tell a classifier you are a broker. The read: every one of the last six posts came back Real Estate as top industry, and the two with the widest out-of-network reach came back Washington DC–Baltimore as top location. The decisive one was not the ghosting story. It was Oct 20, the sponsor-sequence post — no real estate content anywhere in it, and it skewed hardest of all at Real Estate 33% and DC–Baltimore 34%. Content was not steering the audience. The profile was. Changed the same day to “Newsletter operator and systems builder | Building lean digital businesses, and documenting how I do it | 11,000 subscribers, 70%+ opens.” Keyword first, because that is where the classifier reads, and no offer claimed, because New NEXT does not have one defined yet. The caveat to carry: Real Estate tops the small in-network posts too, which means the follower base is itself real estate heavy. That is 31 years in DC, not a headline. The headline moves the classifier. Only the comment routine in section 07 moves the engagement graph. Re-read Feb 1, 2027. The dossier takes about 90 days to rebuild and anything read before then is noise
Newsletters bypass the feed
Noted, not recommended
A LinkedIn newsletter notifies every subscriber directly rather than asking the algorithm for permission, creation is now open to every member, and roughly 28 million people subscribe to at least one. The obvious joke is that you already do this for a living. The reason it is not a recommendation: it is a second publication with its own cadence debt, and you are three posts behind on this one. 04

Video, one a week

Video isn't a fourth lane. It's a format you apply to a lane you're already writing. One post a week becomes video, rotating through the three lanes, so the cadence stays at three and nothing extra gets added to the calendar.

Two findings make this worth the friction. Video reach is climbing 15-25% month over month. And the compounding effect matters more: someone who watches one of your videos gets roughly two extra posts from you in their next 25. So a video doesn't just perform, it buys distribution for the ordinary text posts around it.

Worth noting, since it's easy to miss: van der Blom's own top-performing confession post was a video. 24K impressions, about 40K video views. Video plus insight is the proven pairing, not just video plus teardown. But it's also the hardest to shoot, so the rotation below climbs toward it rather than opening on it.

Length
Under 90 seconds, aim 45-60
Nobody on LinkedIn watches five minutes. The ceiling is 90; the sweet spot is shorter
Captions
Always, burned in
Most feed video is watched muted. No captions means no watch time, and watch time is the whole point
First 3 seconds
The hook, spoken
Same hook discipline as a text post. Open on the claim, not on "hey everyone"
Production
Real over polished
Screen recordings of actual dashboards and talking head at your desk. The algorithm rewards what AI can't fake, and a real artifact on screen is exactly that

The rotation climbs on purpose: screen recording first (lowest friction, you're just narrating something real on your monitor), then a talking head on a low-stakes topic, then another screen recording, then the talking-head confession in Week 4 once you've got three weeks of camera reps behind you.

The experiment post (added Oct 16, from Matthew Brown). Test one idea in your own business, then break down what you tried and what you learned. It works because it shows momentum and execution: anyone can share an opinion, an experiment shows you ran something. Five steps, in order:

  1. One clear hypothesis. One specific idea, kept narrow
  2. The setup. Just enough context to follow the test
  3. The result. Specific numbers, not adjectives
  4. What you learned. What surprised you and what you think caused it
  5. What happens next. The decision the experiment led to

The comment routine

The gap this closes: everything before this section is about publishing. But you have roughly 3,950 followers, most of them legacy contacts from a former career, and no topic cluster built yet. Only 10% of a post's initial distribution goes to your own connections now. So in month one your posts have a low ceiling no matter how good they are. Commenting borrows someone else's distribution instead of waiting to build your own — which is why, for a small account, it is probably the bigger reach lever of the two
The bar
Level 3 or it didn't happen
Likes and "great post" are level 1 and 2 and count for nothing. A real point, data point, or lived experience is the floor. Aim for level 4+ — specific enough that the author replies to you directly, which is where profile visits start
Timing
Posts under 2 hours old
Early comments ride the post's own distribution instead of arriving after it peaked. Same mechanic as your golden hour, viewed from the other side of the table
Schedule
15 min, five days, from the phone
Reshaped Oct 1. Was 20-30 min on the three posting days, at the desk, and it wasn't happening. Same weekly total, spread wider, low enough activation energy to survive a bad week. The reason the habit was light was never discipline: 25 minutes at the desk competes with real work, 15 minutes in a checkout line competes with nothing
Length
Fifteen words is the floor
Added Nov 3. Comments of 15+ words are reportedly valued about 2.5x higher than short ones, because a long comment is richer text for the system to read. And replies to other people’s comments, not just to the post, can carry up to 2.4x more reach than a top-level comment. The thread is worth more than the first comment. This puts a number on the add-don’t-respond rule below: “great point” is not just weak manners, it is almost unreadable to the thing deciding who you are
Reply craft
Add, don't respond
From Alić. The test is whether the comment could stand alone as a post. "Great point" is a wave. A number, a counter-case, or a thing you lived is a contribution. Same for replies on your own posts — a reply that just thanks someone spends a slot that could have carried an argument
Your own thread
Never post and ghost
The 60-90 minutes after you publish is the cheapest reach available, and post #4 went out at 2:52 and was then left alone. Every reply you write is a fresh signal and more dwell time on your own post. The golden hour in section 03 is this rule from the author's side of the table. Keep 10:00 to 10:30 clear after you publish and answer every comment inside that window
Sequencing
Comment first, then post
Run the session 9:30-10, publish at 10. You show up in the right feeds just before your own post lands, you arrive at your own golden hour already warmed up, and you're already typing in-app
Craft
True, additive, and closed with a statement
Start from something you've actually lived. Add what the post didn't say — if anyone who read it could have written your comment, it adds nothing. And end on a statement: every AI comment ends in a question now and readers can smell it
Never automate
Every comment typed by you
An AI-flagged comment loses 88% of its reach impact and leaves a 15% penalty on your next genuine one. The trust score is cumulative, so one shortcut taxes everything after it

The self-pinned comment. Free, and currently unused. Your own comments sit at the top of your own post by default — no feature to turn on, no plugin. Which means every post can carry three or four extra pieces underneath it, each one a separate reason for someone to stop. Four to six is Alić's number; three is enough to start.

They don't need images, they need numbers. The temptation is to treat these as a screenshot gallery, and there is no screenshot gallery — one asset exists, the Sep 17 click-report facsimile, and it's held for a post built around it. A pinned comment is one figure and one sentence: Meta's reported $0.75 per subscriber against the verified $1.13. 71.1% open against a 6.75% click rate. 348 of 968 clicks on a single story.

Write them as the post goes live, inside the first few minutes, so they're in place before anyone arrives. They also solve a real drafting problem: the figure that got cut for length now has somewhere to go instead of making the post longer.

Who to comment on, and the trap. The obvious list is the creators you already study — van der Blom, Welsh, Koerner, Bustamante, Bush. Those are peers, and peer posts are where the big-reach threads are, so they're worth some of your time. But Newsletter #70's warning applies here too: peer surfaces earn you peer recognition, not buyers. Spend at least half the routine where the people who'd actually hire you are — operators running small media and commerce businesses, newsletter people, solo founders wiring their own systems. Fewer impressions per comment, far better ones.

The 1:1 layer — the half of this plan that plays to your actual strength. Everything else here is broadcast: posting is one-to-many, commenting is public. None of it uses the thing you spent 31 years getting good at. The New NEXT thesis is digital stack plus business development, and so far the plan is all stack.

The practice: when someone engages meaningfully — a real comment on your post, or a genuine exchange on theirs — move it to a message. Reference the specific thing they said. Ask something you actually want to know. No pitch, ever, in a first message. Two or three a week, not two hundred connection requests.

You've already built this instinct into a system: your sponsor pipeline refuses to send a personal email until someone clicks or replies, because you'd learned that pouncing on a lukewarm lead kills it. Same rule, different surface. The difference between this and what everyone else is doing is that you're reaching out after someone raised their hand, with no ask attached

A gap this section has, named Nov 3. Publishing, commenting and the occasional DM are all the distribution this plan currently has, and all three are you reaching people one at a time. There is no partner play. The people already talking to the operators you would serve — other local-newsletter publishers, the Beehiiv and Matt McGarry orbits, podcast hosts who interview people running small media businesses — are non-competitive with you and have the audience you are trying to reach. One of them mentioning you is worth more than a month of comments. This is not a month-one play and it is not an outreach list. It is a channel the plan does not currently have, recorded so it stops being invisible.

Post selection beats comment craft. His level-6 showcase sits on a Gary Vaynerchuk post with 639 comments — most of those 19,859 impressions are Vaynerchuk's audience, not the comment's brilliance. Comment impressions scale with the host post's reach, so a great comment on a quiet post earns nothing. Pick posts that are already moving, in your topic, under two hours old. Then write well

The operating layer

From CE-007. The lanes say what to write and the checklist says how to build it; this is the part that decides whether any of it actually happens on a busy week. Bustamante's own working rhythm, adapted down to your cadence.

Time budget
3 protected hours a week
His real number, and it's the whole budget — once or twice a week as deep work, not scattered. 20% on ideation (getting ideas out of your head), 80% on craft: angle, hook, image, CTA. You don't need to live on LinkedIn
Triage
The 30-second test
Inside the block: if you can't picture the hook and feel a small hit of interest within 30 seconds, kill the idea and move on. If you can, spend the ~30 minutes and write it. Stops you grinding on ideas that were never going to land
CTA split
2 with, 1 without
Of the week's three posts, two carry a CTA and one is pure reach with no link at all. That's the reach-versus-conversion portfolio made concrete, so you're not deciding post by post
The drop
One every 3-4 weeks
Revised Oct 13. A hook-plus-image post teasing a free asset, delivered openly: the asset itself in your own comment, or a conversion post with the link in the body. No asking for a comment and no DMing commenters, which is the keyword CTA LinkedIn is now penalizing. Make the first one a book curation, with the list itself as your first comment. Zero new IP, and you learn the pipe end to end before building a real asset
Always-on
Featured, one magnet
Your best gated asset in Featured, treated as a thumbnail, not a bare "subscribe" link. Ship it once, benefit forever. (The custom profile button is the other one, but confirm it still exists before paying for Premium on its account)
Idea engine
Mine your calendar
Twelve briefs run out. The renewable source: what conversations did you have yesterday, last week, five years ago? What did people ask you? What problems did you solve? That's the strategy, and it's AI-proof because nobody else lived it. Calendar generates candidates, the 30-second test filters them
Hook template
Moment + credibility + outcome + secret
"Last week [moment] we helped a client [credibility] drive [outcome] using a tactic I discovered back [secret]." Fills the Hook and Positioning slots at once, and the credibility half stays recent and specific rather than becoming a credential flex

The six-element checklist

Run every post through this before it goes out. The lanes decide what you write; this decides the order, which van der Blom's August teardown argues is worth more than the topic. The two aren't competing — the six elements are the skeleton, Authority ÷ Insight is the entry point that fills slot 4.

0 · The moment
Find the scene before the hook
Upstream of everything else. A specific moment with a time and a place — the Wednesday you looked at noon and the issue was already drafted, the Tuesday you pulled the click report. Magali De Reu audits posts for a living and says almost all of them fail here, not at the hook: no real moment, and every step after it collapses. If you're reaching for a clever opener, you're missing a scene
1 · Hook
Lead with a number
Ranked by 6,316 posts: statistic 1.83x (used in only 10% of top posts), product news 1.72x, story 1.21x, direct address 1.19x, contrarian 1.16x. Question 0.88x and plain observation 0.87x — the two to avoid, and the plain observation is what 59.5% of posts default into. Never open with a question; save it for the CTE. Nov 3 recut: a personal first line scores 1.27x and the question opener 0.93x, close enough to the older ranking to leave it standing. Your best opener is both at once — post #1’s “Sixty-one people clicked from CHS Happenings, my Charleston newsletter…” is a statistic and a personal first line in one sentence. And mind the cut: two line breaks near the top can end the feed preview at about 60 characters, so keep the opening lines together
2 · Promise
What they get for staying
A "see more" click is the benefit of the doubt, not won attention. If you can't say the promise in one line, the post doesn't have one yet
3 · Positioning
Proximity, never the bio
Your sentence-level fix for default gravity. Not "31 years in commercial real estate." Instead: "I've shipped this newsletter every Thursday for two years to 11,000 people." Present tense, proof by doing. If everybody could have written it, it builds no trust
4 · Core Message
About 70% of the post
Where the value lands. Make the point with a punch and be directive. No jargon, and don't assume they know what you mean
5 · Conclusion
Land it without repeating it
If the last line summarises what you just said, cut it. Reframe into a principle instead of restating the story
6 · Call to Engage
A question, not a pitch
Decides whether the post ends in a scroll or a conversation. Best design: ask a question whose answer reveals a problem the reader has. Self-diagnostic beats generic. It is a question, never an instruction: no "drop your thoughts," "let me know," "save this" or "share with someone who," and no hint of a sales follow-up. Sharpened Nov 1: any ask now runs -20%, the polite closing question included. The version that survives is self-directed — point the reader at their own last ten posts, their own numbers, their own week, with nothing in the sentence implying they owe you an answer. If they reply, that was their idea

The winning hook is the one you're best equipped to write. The statistic hook tops the ranking and almost nobody uses it, because most people don't have real numbers. You do: 11,000 subscribers, 70% opens, 7% clickthrough against a 1.7% industry average, a platform over-reporting your ad results by about 50% ($0.75 claimed against $1.13 verified, Apr 4 to Aug 9 2026), and a list that loses about 130 subscribers a week whether or not you notice. Three separate frameworks now converge on the same instruction — the hook ranking says lead with a number, Positioning says proximity over credential, and the three-question test says a cost with a number attached. Open with your real figures. Post #1 did exactly this — it opened on "Sixty-one people clicked…", a verified figure from your own weekly report.

Hold the claim loosely. His 1.8x reach and 2.5x conversion come with no n, no control, and no definition of conversion — he pattern-matched his own winners, which is survivorship. The post is also a waitlist pitch for a €149 playbook. Use the six elements as a craft checklist, not a proven multiplier

The reports that find me  ·  Week 2 Authority. Kept here because the drafting fight was more instructive than the post: four separate rounds of catching claims that weren't quite true

Every number I need is one click away. For two years, that's exactly why I never looked.

Beehiiv, Google Analytics, Meta Ads Manager, Airtable, Stripe, Kit. All of them, all perfectly good. All one click away.

I go look when I'm curious.

That turned out to be the whole problem.

You're only curious about the things that are going well. The number you're avoiding is the one you never go looking for.

I spent two years believing my newsletter fed my real estate business. I never once checked. Not because checking was hard. Because checking was something I had to decide to do, and I never decided.

So I stopped relying on places to go, and built things that show up instead.

Five of them now, on a schedule, whether I want them or not:

Every morning: what's on today, what came in overnight, what needs an answer.

Every Tuesday: what readers actually clicked in last week's issue, every link ranked, plus where the website traffic came from. This is the one that told me the truth I didn't want.

Every week: how each running sponsor's campaign is performing, in the same format I send them. If it's a down week, I know before they do.

Every week: the state of both businesses. Pipeline, deadlines, what's gone quiet.

Every month: who my subscribers actually are, recomputed from active readers only. Not the flattering all-time number.

None of these are real-time. None of them are pretty for the sake of being pretty. They arrive as a page I read in four minutes with coffee.

The rule, if you're building your own: if a number only reaches you when you go get it, you will stop going to get it. Put it on a schedule and let it find you.

What still isn't automated is deciding what any of it means. That part has never once been the bottleneck.

What's the number you check least often? And is that because it doesn't matter, or because you'd rather not see it?

Four things got caught in drafting, and every one was the same failure: a line that sounded good and wasn't quite true. (2) "If it's a bad week" — CHS has down weeks, never bad ones. Not softening: "bad" invites a refund conversation from a sponsor, "down" invites a next-week conversation. Voice, punctuation, and who drafts

Settled Sep 29 mid-draft on post #4, extended Oct 1. The drafting rule was revised Oct 13 and is now a two-week trial.

The target voice is the CHS Happenings voice, not a warmer version of analytical. Witty, a little cheeky, insider-y, unembarrassed about having an opinion. It already exists, it already works on eleven thousand people weekly, and it needs no invention here. The only translation is subject: CHS Happenings is that voice pointed at a city, LinkedIn is that voice pointed at your own operation. Same temperature. Nov 3 put numbers on this: simple, spoken-sounding posts take 1.24x reach and up to 40% more comments, and his one-line test is the read-aloud test this plan already runs — if you would not say it out loud, do not post it.

This is also how the Like lane gets solved without writing a single "personal" post. Nobody good writes personal posts. They're recognisably themselves while teaching, and the register takes care of itself.

The one adjustment. The CHS voice is cheeky about Charleston, which is a safe target. The version that works here is cheeky about your own machinery. Post #5 is the test case: a platform billed you for 1,047 subscribers that don't exist and you caught it. There is a funny version of that story and the draft on the bench is the audit version.

The first draft is Jimmy's own words, typed or spoken. Every post, no exceptions. Dictate it into Wispr Flow or type it ugly: three hundred words, no hook, whatever comes out. Claude never writes a first draft from nothing. On a dictated draft Claude may ask up to three questions, trim to about 250 words, reorder, flag claims the story does not support, run the never-list and score the hook. It adds no sentences, jokes or facts. Jimmy rewrites it and types every sentence into LinkedIn by hand. Posts #1, #2 and #4 were drafted for him and re-voiced; #3 he wrote himself first, and it's the one that sounds most like him.

Why it changed (Oct 13). The blank page was the blocker, not who types. Jimmy had stopped posting because he was torn between writing every draft himself and letting Claude draft with his input. Charlie Hills' Claude-heavy method and Richard van der Blom's data were weighed against his own record: the reach penalty for AI help is small, and the real risks are voice drift and invented facts. Speaking keeps the words his and removes the blank page. Restarted Oct 15, after the announcement: six posts, Oct 15 to Oct 28, reviewed Thu Oct 29.

Hook coaching, first three months (Oct 13, 2026 to Jan 13, 2027). The hook is the first gate and the hardest line to write cold, so Claude helps draft it. From the dictated draft, Claude offers three to five hook options, each labeled by type (statistic, story, direct address, contrarian) with a one-line reason, and built only from words and facts that are in the transcript. Anything not in the transcript is flagged, never filled in: post #4's four slips all came from reaching for a punchier line. Claude also says what makes a hook work, so the skill transfers. Jimmy picks one, rewrites it in his own voice and types it in. Reviewed Jan 13.

Claude reviews a draft in this order: 1) the hook: does it earn the see-more, and which type is it; 2) a closing question that makes a stranger describe their own problem; 3) lane fit and Know / Trust / Like register; 4) voice and the never-list, including the read-aloud test; 5) every number and claim traced to something Jimmy said or saw. The trial is judged on posts shipped, recognition comments and signups, not impressions. Each post is logged with a "drafted by" field.

The failure mode this guards against: writing is the hard part, so it's the part that gets handed off first, and handing it off feels productive. Same shape as the eighty days. On a day with nothing drafted and no time, run a bench post — one already thought through — rather than generating from cold.

Every post gets an outline first (standing rule, Oct 15). For the first 30 days and every week after, each slot comes with an outline: the angle, the beats worth covering in order, what the hook has to do, three questions to dictate against, the format, what to watch, and the direction of the closing question. It exists because the blank page was the blocker.

What an outline is not. It is not a draft. It holds no sentences to paste, no jokes and no facts that did not come from Jimmy's own briefs, numbers or words, and anything unconfirmed is marked as a gate. The first draft is still his own words, typed or spoken, and the hook options still come only from his transcript. Outlines for new slots are written when a slot opens, not weeks ahead, so they carry current facts.

No em dashes in LinkedIn posts. Period breaks instead, and a colon where a list or a continuation follows. The draft of post #4 carried eight em dashes, five of them doing the identical job in consecutive lines. That density is the tell, not the mark itself.

Don't over-correct into the other cliché. A period after every clause produces the staccato LinkedIn-guru cadence, which is just as recognisable. The fix is variety, not swapping one mark for another everywhere.

Colons on parallel list items are an upgrade, not a substitution. On the five report lines, a colon signals "here comes the content" more cleanly than a dash does. Worth reaching for first anywhere the post enumerates.
