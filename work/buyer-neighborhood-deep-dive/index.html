It sits in a drainage basin with a documented flooding history

After repeated flooding, the City and FEMA bought and demolished homes inside this neighborhood and turned the land into open space. The City has also spent thirty years engineering against the problem, with work finished as recently as last year. Both halves are true, and both are laid out below.

04Your budget here

A $1.2 million budget is above the top of this market

Buying at the top of the neighborhood is the expensive way to do it.

Paying $1.2M where the proven ceiling is $980,000 means being over-improved from the day you close, and that gap doesn't go away at resale. The efficient version: buy in the $650,000 to $750,000 range on a lot that takes a pool, and put the remaining $450,000 to $550,000 into the pool, the outdoor space and the renovation you said you're open to. We'd rather you hear the ceiling from us than from an appraiser.

05The flooding record

What happened, and what the City did about it

1990sNew homes in one section flooded in several storms. The City found standard stormwater design "not effective in this drainage basin" and paused building for nine months.
2015 · 2016 · 2017Three major floods in a row. A later newspaper investigation found damaged homes the City's list had missed.
2019FEMA and the City bought out 40 properties in and next to the neighborhood and turned the land into permanent green space.
2001 to 2025Thirty years of fixes: a calibrated basin model, new storage, a diversion canal and culverts, stricter basin-specific rules in 2020, and street-level improvements finished in 2025. A former townhome site is now a six-acre ecological park that holds stormwater.
The finding that matters most to a buyer

Privacy law can make it nearly impossible to know whether a house flooded before if the seller doesn't say. And a 2002 study found homes here flooded despite not being in FEMA flood zones at all. A clean map designation is evidence, not proof.

What we couldn't verify

Some big numbers circulating online, like a $44 million project total and a $26.6 million pump station, we couldn't source. We're not repeating them as fact. We'll confirm with the City floodplain office instead.

06Before an offer

What we'd verify on any address here

  1. Screen against the buyout streetsBefore scheduling a showing, not after.
  2. Order the City's flood letter for the addressThe authoritative answer, not the public map lookup.
  3. Ask in writing if it has ever taken waterA written question gets a written answer.
  4. Pull the elevation certificate and insurance historyClaims follow the property.
  5. Look at the lotWhere it sits against the ditches, ponds and golf course drainage matters more here than almost anywhere.
